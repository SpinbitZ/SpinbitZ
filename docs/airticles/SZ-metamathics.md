A. Traditional approaches to metamathematics

### 1\. Logicism, formalism, and intuitionism

The landscape of 20th-century metamathematics has been dominated by three major philosophical programs: logicism, formalism, and intuitionism. Each represents a distinct approach to the foundations of mathematics, yet all share certain assumptions that Morrison's work ultimately challenges.

Logicism, initiated by Frege and developed by Russell and Whitehead, sought to reduce mathematics to logic, thereby securing its foundations on what was considered the most fundamental form of reasoning. The logicist program aimed to demonstrate that mathematical truths are analytic---true by virtue of their logical form alone---rather than synthetic or dependent on experience. Despite its initial promise, logicism encountered significant challenges, most notably Russell's paradox, which demonstrated the inconsistency of naive set theory and necessitated increasingly complex systems of types and axioms.

Formalism, championed by Hilbert, took a different approach by treating mathematics as a game played with meaningless symbols according to precisely defined rules. In this view, mathematical truth consists solely in the consistency of the formal system, with no necessary reference to any external reality. Hilbert's program sought to establish the consistency of mathematics through finitary methods, a goal that was ultimately shown to be impossible by Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems, which demonstrated that any consistent formal system rich enough to express elementary arithmetic must contain statements that cannot be proven or disproven within that system.

Intuitionism, developed by Brouwer and later Heyting, rejected both the logicist reduction of mathematics to logic and the formalist separation of mathematics from meaning. For intuitionists, mathematics is fundamentally a human mental construction, and mathematical truth consists in what can be mentally constructed or intuited by a finite mind. This led to a rejection of certain classical principles, such as the law of excluded middle, and a restriction of mathematical entities to those that could be constructed through finitely many steps.

While these three approaches differ significantly in their understanding of mathematical truth and existence, Morrison identifies several common limitations that characterize traditional metamathematics as a whole:

First, all three approaches operate within what Morrison terms a "transitive" framework---focusing on the horizontal relationships of logical implication and formal derivation rather than the vertical relationships of increasing integration and complexity that constitute the "immanent-transcendent axis."

Second, all three approaches struggle with the concept of infinity, either by attempting to eliminate it through finitistic methods (formalism), restricting it to potential rather than actual infinity (intuitionism), or formalizing it in ways that obscure its ontological significance (logicism).

Third, each approach maintains some version of the subject-object distinction that Morrison's nondual perspective seeks to transcend---whether by positing mathematical objects as independent logical entities (logicism), as arbitrary symbols manipulated according to rules (formalism), or as mental constructions distinct from the external world (intuitionism).

Within the context of these broader philosophical approaches, two mathematical frameworks have emerged as particularly influential in contemporary metamathematics: set theory and category theory.

Set theory, particularly in its axiomatic form as developed by Zermelo, Fraenkel, and others, has served as the de facto foundation for most of modern mathematics. By reducing mathematical entities to sets and mathematical operations to set-theoretical operations, axiomatic set theory provides a unified framework within which diverse mathematical structures can be defined and analyzed. The success of this approach has been so complete that many mathematicians take set-theoretical foundations for granted, rarely questioning the ontological assumptions embedded in this reduction.

Morrison's critique of set-theoretical foundations focuses on what he sees as its artificial linearization of inherently multidimensional concepts. By reducing all mathematical entities to sets, set theory imposes a "flattening" that obscures the natural dimensional relationships between mathematical concepts. Furthermore, the set-theoretical treatment of infinity through Cantor's transfinite cardinals, while mathematically powerful, fails to capture what Morrison sees as the ontological significance of infinity as the "quantitative face of the absolute."

Category theory, emerging in the mid-20th century through the work of Eilenberg, Mac Lane, and others, offers an alternative foundational framework focused on mathematical structures and the relationships (morphisms) between them rather than on their constituent elements. By emphasizing relationships over elements, category theory aligns more closely with Morrison's emphasis on relational thinking. However, Morrison argues that even category theory, with its focus on horizontal relationships between structures, fails to fully incorporate the vertical or "immanent-transcendent" dimension that is central to his approach.

In both set theory and category theory, Morrison identifies a preference for abstract formal definitions over visual-intuitive understanding. This relegation of the visual to secondary status reflects what Morrison, drawing on Arnheim, describes as the "sleeping" of our "inborn capacity to understand through the eyes."

### 3\. The persistent dualism in mathematical philosophy

Despite their differences, traditional approaches to metamathematics share a persistent dualism that Morrison's nondual perspective aims to overcome. This dualism manifests in several related dichotomies:

The dichotomy between syntax and semantics---between the formal symbols of mathematics and their meaning or interpretation---remains a central feature of conventional metamathematics. Whether this dichotomy is resolved in favor of syntax (as in formalism), semantics (as in various forms of mathematical realism), or through some correspondence between the two (as in model theory), the underlying assumption of their separability persists.

Similarly, the dichotomy between the discrete and the continuous---between countable collections of distinct elements and uncountable continua---has structured mathematical thought since ancient times. Modern mathematics has developed sophisticated tools for working with both discrete and continuous structures, but the relationship between them remains problematic within conventional frameworks.

Perhaps most fundamentally, traditional metamathematics maintains a dichotomy between the knower and the known---between the mathematician as subject and mathematics as object. Even in constructivist approaches that emphasize the role of human activity in creating mathematical structures, this subject-object distinction typically remains intact.

Morrison argues that these persistent dualisms reflect a deeper issue in Western philosophical thought: the failure to recognize the nondual nature of reality and the artificial separation of what are actually poles of a unified spectrum. His work seeks to overcome these dualisms not through a dialectical synthesis that preserves their opposition at a higher level, but through a recognition of their fundamental nonduality---their status as emergent distinctions within a more basic unity.

### 1\. Post-Kantian separation of epistemology from ontology

In mathematics, this post-Kantian separation manifests in several ways. First, it encourages a focus on the epistemological status of mathematical knowledge (how we know mathematical truths) at the expense of the ontological status of mathematical entities (what mathematical objects are). Second, it promotes a view of mathematics as primarily concerned with structures of human understanding rather than with reality itself. Third, it establishes artificial boundaries between mathematical and empirical knowledge, treating them as categorically distinct rather than as different expressions of the same underlying process.

Morrison's critique aims to expose these unexamined ontological assumptions and to demonstrate how they have limited contemporary metamathematics. Morrison extends his critique of the "ontic shadow" to postmodern approaches to mathematics, arguing that they perpetuate and in some ways intensify the ontological avoidance characteristic of post-Kantian philosophy. In postmodern approaches to mathematics, this performative negation typically takes the form of an explicit rejection of metaphysical or ontological claims about mathematics, accompanied by an implicit reliance on unexamined ontological assumptions. The social constructivist view that mathematics is merely a human cultural construction paradoxically relies on unexplained assumptions about the nature of human minds, social processes, and the relationship between language and reality---all fundamentally ontological matters.

The linguistic turn in philosophy of mathematics, which treats mathematical statements as moves in a language game rather than as descriptions of reality, tacitly assumes an ontology in which language is somehow separate from and prior to the reality it supposedly constructs.

The emphasis on mathematics as a historically contingent practice fails to account for the remarkable stability and cross-cultural effectiveness of mathematical concepts---a stability that suggests something more than mere convention is at work.

For Morrison, these postmodern approaches represent not an overcoming of traditional metaphysics but its unconscious continuation in an inverted form. By explicitly rejecting ontological questions while implicitly making ontological assumptions, they create what Morrison calls an "ontic shadow"---a blind spot in philosophical discourse that prevents certain fundamental questions from being addressed.

The "ontic shadow" cast by post-Kantian and postmodern approaches has, according to Morrison, profound consequences for mathematical understanding. Morrison's work seeks to overcome these limitations by developing a metamathematical framework that reintegrates ontology with epistemology, recognizes the dimensional depth of mathematical concepts, and accounts for the relationship between mathematics and reality without resorting either to Platonic realism or to constructivist relativism. In mathematical terms, modal infinity corresponds to what Cantor formalized as the transfinite hierarchies---specific infinities that can be mathematically determined and compared with each other. Morrison argues that the concept of modal infinity provides a philosophical context for understanding the paradoxes associated with infinity in mathematics. By maintaining this distinction, the apparent contradictions can be understood as reflecting the partial or limited expression of the absolute in modal forms.

This interpretation challenges conventional approaches to the paradoxes of infinity, which typically seek either to eliminate infinity altogether (finitism) or to tame it through formalization (standard set theory). The relationship between absolute and modal infinity in Morrison's framework is not one of separation but of expression.
